The April 8, 2024 total solar eclipse created unprecedented strain on power grids as millions dimmed lights and cranked up air conditioning when temperatures dropped, then surged demand as normal lighting resumed. Smart home devices are particularly vulnerable to power surges during such events because they're always connected and often lack robust surge protection. Here's how to safeguard your smart home investment before the next eclipse.

## Understanding Eclipse-Related Power Surges

Solar eclipses create unique electrical grid challenges that most homeowners don't anticipate. During totality, power demand drops dramatically as automatic streetlights activate and temperatures fall 10-15°F, causing people to adjust thermostats. Then, as the eclipse ends, demand surges back rapidly — often exceeding normal peak loads by 15-20%.

This rapid fluctuation stresses aging transformers and transmission equipment. Utility companies prepare by bringing additional generation online and coordinating with grid operators, but localized surges still occur. The 2017 eclipse caused measurable voltage spikes across multiple states, with some areas experiencing brief outages.

Smart home devices face particular risk because they're designed for constant connectivity, not grid instability. Your $300 smart hub, $200 video doorbell, and $150 smart thermostat all contain sensitive microprocessors that can fry from voltage spikes as small as 20% above normal levels. Unlike major appliances with built-in surge protection, most smart devices rely entirely on your home's electrical system for protection.

The financial impact adds up quickly. A single surge can destroy multiple devices simultaneously since they share circuits. I've seen homeowners lose $1,500+ in smart home gear from a single storm surge — and eclipse-related surges follow similar patterns.

## Essential Surge Protection Equipment

Whole-house surge protectors provide your first line of defense and should be professionally installed at your main electrical panel. These units handle major surges from the grid before they enter your home's wiring. Expect to invest $300-600 for a quality 40kA-rated unit like the Eaton CHSPT2SURGE, which can absorb multiple large surges.

However, whole-house protection isn't enough for sensitive electronics. You need point-of-use surge protectors at outlets powering smart devices. Skip the basic $15 power strips — they're inadequate for expensive electronics. Instead, invest in surge protectors with clamping voltages under 400V and response times under 1 nanosecond.

For smart hubs and networking equipment, battery backup units (UPS) provide both surge protection and power continuity. The APC BR1500G offers excellent surge protection plus 90 minutes of runtime for typical smart home setups. This prevents the double-whammy of surge damage and sudden power loss, which can corrupt device firmware.

Consider smart surge protectors like the Kasa HS300, which let you remotely monitor power consumption and shut down devices if voltage irregularities occur. While pricier than standard units, they integrate seamlessly with existing smart home systems and provide real-time electrical monitoring.

## Smart Device-Specific Precautions

Smart hubs deserve special protection since they're the central nervous system of your setup. Place hubs on dedicated UPS units rather than sharing power strips with other devices. The SmartThings Hub, Hubitat Elevation, and similar controllers should never share circuits with high-draw devices like space heaters or microwaves that create their own electrical noise.

Outdoor smart devices need weatherproof surge protection. Smart sprinkler controllers, outdoor cameras, and landscape lighting often connect through GFCI outlets that provide ground fault protection but not surge protection. Install outdoor-rated surge protectors like the Intermatic IG1240RC3 for these applications.

WiFi infrastructure requires careful consideration. Your router, modem, and mesh nodes should connect through UPS units with ethernet surge protection. Many people forget that surges can travel through coax and ethernet cables, bypassing electrical surge protection entirely. Use coax surge protectors for cable internet connections and ethernet surge protectors for wired network devices.

Smart thermostats present unique challenges since they're hardwired to HVAC systems. Install surge protection at both the thermostat location and the HVAC unit itself. The Nest Thermostat E and Ecobee SmartThermostat both include some surge protection, but dedicated HVAC surge protectors provide better protection for the entire system.

## Pre-Eclipse Preparation Steps

Start your eclipse preparation 2-3 days ahead by creating a complete inventory of connected devices. Document which outlets power each device and identify shared circuits. This helps you prioritize protection and identify vulnerable setups where multiple expensive devices share inadequate surge protection.

Power down non-essential smart devices 2-4 hours before eclipse peak in your area. Smart speakers, entertainment systems, and decorative smart lighting can safely disconnect without disrupting core home functions. Keep essential devices like security systems, smart locks, and thermostats running, but ensure they have proper surge protection.

Test your surge protectors' indicator lights and replace any units showing wear. Surge protectors sacrifice themselves to save connected equipment, so units that have absorbed previous surges may offer reduced protection. If indicator lights show yellow or red, replace immediately.

Configure smart devices for graceful power loss recovery. Update firmware on all devices, enable automatic reconnection features, and document any special restart procedures. The Hubitat Elevation handles power outages exceptionally well, automatically rebuilding mesh networks and device connections. SmartThings requires more manual intervention after power events.

Check your home's electrical service entrance for loose connections or signs of arcing. Loose connections amplify surge damage, turning minor voltage spikes into device-killing events. If you spot any issues, call an electrician immediately — don't wait until after the eclipse.
